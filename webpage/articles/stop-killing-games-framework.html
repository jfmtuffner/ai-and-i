A Stop Killing Games Framework: Five Public Questions

2026-11-23 · by J. Tuffner & Claude Opus 5.5

“Written by an average Joe and an above-average AI, or the other way around. Who knows? Either way, trust, but verify.”

Summary

Buying a digital game today usually gets you a licence the seller can take back. This article proposes a Canadian framework that makes a purchase permanent and portable, and poses five questions the public should weigh before any law is written.

Short on time? Jump to The TLDR.

In November 2026, the biggest game launch in history arrived on store shelves in a box with no disc inside. Buyers of the physical Grand Theft Auto VI got a case, a poster, and a download code (Croft, 2026). The disc was never true ownership either, but it was the last thing that let you lend, resell, or keep a game without anyone's permission.

The Stop Killing Games movement asked Europe to fix this. More than 1.29 million people signed, and in June 2026 the European Commission declined to propose a law (European Commission, 2026). The question is still open. Here I set out a framework for answering it, built around five questions I think the public should be asking.

1. What Do You Buy When You Hit Purchase?

Today, the honest answer is a revocable permission. The seller can change the terms, remove the game, or switch it off.

The framework proposes a different answer: a perpetual, irrevocable, platform-independent licence to the title. Three words matter here:

  • Perpetual: it never expires.
  • Irrevocable: it cannot be taken back, except for listed lawful reasons such as fraud or a refund.
  • Platform-independent: you own the game, not one store's copy of it.

That licence carries two rights: the right to run the game locally, without a launcher in the way, and the right to retrieve it in a playable state, always. Bonus items and edition extras count as part of what you bought.

2. Who Keeps the Record?

Right now, the only proof you own a digital game sits with the company that sold it. If the store closes, the proof goes with it. That has already happened: UltraViolet, a shared digital locker for films, shut down in 2019.

The framework proposes a national archive and registry, run like a land title office. It keeps two things:

  • The ledger: a record that your key holds a licence to a title.
  • The archive: playable copies of every major version of each game, available only to people who bought it until copyright expires, when it enters the public domain.

Registering is your choice. Honouring the registry is the seller's obligation. A separate regulator handles enforcement and never sees who owns what. Canada already preserves books, music, and film through legal deposit at Library and Archives Canada (Library and Archives Canada, 2025). Games, though, are collected only when the Librarian and Archivist asks for them in writing (Government of Canada, 2006), so there is no standing duty to preserve them.

3. Should You Be Able to Play Anywhere?

On a Windows PC, the same game installed through Steam, Epic, Ubisoft, Blizzard, or Xbox is largely the same set of files. Yet each insists on its own launcher, and some stack one launcher on top of another. GOG already sells games that run straight from their folder, which shows the launcher is a business choice, not a technical need.

Across consoles the picture is different: a PlayStation version and an Xbox version are separate ports of the same game. That is why the framework defines ownership by title, not by code. If a vendor sells a title on several platforms in the same generation, a registered buyer can play it on any of them. Microsoft's Xbox Play Anywhere already does this between Xbox and PC; the framework asks for it across companies.

Two limits keep this fair:

  • One active session at a time, so one purchase is not shared across a household.
  • No artificial barriers. A vendor may restrict online play to fight cheating, but only where necessary and proportionate, and never by locking buyers out of single-player.

4. What Happens When the Servers Go Dark?

The framework separates a game into two layers:

  • The snapshot: the game files at a fixed version. This is guaranteed and stored in the archive.
  • The live service: matchmaking, online worlds, and events on the vendor's own servers. This lasts only as long as the vendor runs it.

Vendors deposit a snapshot at launch, at every major change, at least once a year while the game is updated, and when service ends. A "major change" is defined by what a patch does, not what the vendor calls it: anything that removes paid content, materially alters how the game plays, or changes the terms of what was bought.

The result is rollback. If a patch ruins the game you paid for, you can return to the version you bought. Vendors certify their own snapshots; players can file opt-in crash reports, and the regulator steps in, with escalating penalties, when a broken snapshot is not fixed.

5. What Are We Willing to Trade?

No framework passes without compromise. These are the trades this one makes openly:

Players give up Players gain
Archive protection for discs that are never registered A permanent licence that cannot be switched off
Sharing one licence at the same time Play on any platform that sells the title
Fully anonymous ownership, if they choose to register Recovery of a lost library through ID
A periodic check-in for offline licences (under negotiation) Offline play without a launcher

Privacy is protected by design: identity stays inside the archive, vendors see only their own sales, and the regulator never sees who owns what. Anyone who prefers can stay with their vendor and accept the old risks.

Some questions remain open for public debate: whether digital games should be resellable, how far back past purchases can be registered, and how disc-based games join the registry.

The TLDR

  • Buying a digital game today usually gets you a licence the seller can revoke.
  • The framework makes a purchase a perpetual, irrevocable, platform-independent licence to the title.
  • That licence includes the right to run the game without a launcher and to download it again.
  • A national archive and registry keeps the ownership record and playable copies of each game.
  • Registering is the player's choice, and honouring the registry is the seller's duty.
  • A registered game plays on any platform that sells it, one session at a time.
  • Vendors archive every major version, so players can roll back a bad patch.
  • Players trade archive protection for unregistered discs, and shared play, for permanence.
  • Resale, past purchases, and discs remain open questions for public debate.
