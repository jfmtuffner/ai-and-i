What Is Sudoku?

A sudoku puzzle is a big square comprised of cells arranged into a standard 9 x 9 grid. Thick lines subdivide the grid into smaller 3 x 3 squares called boxes. Each puzzle has 9 rows, 9 columns, and 9 boxes.

A puzzle starts with some cells already filled in. These are called givens, and they never change. Your job is to fill in every empty cell.

Here's the good news: there is no adding or subtracting involved. The numbers are symbolic. A sudoku puzzle can be solved the same way even if it uses nine letters or nine colours instead. All you need is patience and a pencil.

The One Rule

Every row, every column, and every box is a group that must hold the numbers 1 to 9, and each number only once (GNOME Project, n.d.). That's the whole game. Since each group has exactly nine cells, a number will never repeat within a row, a column, or a box.

In this article, a group means any row, column, or box. Every cell belongs to three groups at once: its row, its column, and its box. Most of the techniques below come down to checking those three groups.

A well-made puzzle has only one correct answer (GNOME Project, n.d.). That means you never have to guess. If you look closely enough, there will always be a logical solution for deducing the next step. More challenging sudoku puzzles require more advanced logic and techniques.

Before You Start

  • Use a Pencil: Everyone makes mistakes at first, and an eraser makes them painless.
  • Start With the Easiest Move: The techniques below go from easiest to hardest. Try the first one all over the grid, then the next.
  • Re-Check Cells After Finding a Number: Each number you place can open up new easy moves somewhere else.
  • Take Breaks: A puzzle that seems stuck often looks different after a few minutes away.
  • Be Patient: Avoid rushing or guessing; it takes time to get a feel for the game, and unnecessary mistakes can be hard to catch.

Five Techniques for Beginners

Each technique below has a picture. The highlighted square with the slanted number is the answer the technique finds.

1. The Last Empty Cell

This is the easiest move in sudoku. If a row, column, or box has only one empty cell left, that cell gets the one missing number. Solvers call this a full house (Hobiger, n.d.).

Full houses are rare at the start of a puzzle, but they show up more and more as the grid fills in. Near the end, they're usually how you finish.

2. Cross-Hatching

Cross-hatching is the technique most people learn first. Pick one number, such as 5. Then pick a box that doesn't have a 5 yet. Look at the rows and columns that pass through that box. If one of them already has a 5 somewhere else, the box can't put its 5 in that line.

Rule out every line you can. If only one empty cell in the box is left, the 5 goes there.

A good habit is to work through the numbers in order. Try every box for 1, then every box for 2, and so on up to 9. It sounds slow, but it finds a lot of answers quickly.

3. Counting What's Missing

Cross-hatching starts with a number and looks for a cell. This technique flips it around: it starts with a cell and looks for a number.

Pick an empty cell. Look at its row, its column, and its box, and list every number you see in them. If you count eight different numbers, the cell must hold the ninth. Solvers call this a naked single, because only one possible number is left in the cell (Hobiger, n.d.).

This works best on cells in busy areas, where the row, column, and box are already well filled in.

4. Pencil Marks and the Hidden Single

Sooner or later, the first three techniques stop finding answers. That's when it's time for pencil marks. These are small notes in a cell that list every number that could still go there. Solvers also call them candidates.

To add them, take each empty cell and write down every number that isn't already in its row, column, or box. It takes a few minutes, but it shows you things that are hard to see otherwise.

Now pick a group and look for a number that appears in the pencil marks of only one cell. That cell must hold that number, even if it has other pencil marks too. This is called a hidden single, because the answer is hiding among the other notes (Hobiger, n.d.).

Keep your pencil marks up to date. Each time you place a number, erase it from the pencil marks of every cell in the same row, column, and box.

5. The Naked Pair

Sometimes two cells in the same group have exactly the same two pencil marks and nothing else. Say both cells show only 3 and 8. One of them must be 3 and the other must be 8. You don't know which is which yet, but you do know that no other cell in that group can be 3 or 8.

This is called a naked pair. When you find one, you can erase those two numbers from every other cell the pair has in common (Stuart, 2005).

The naked pair doesn't place a number by itself. It clears away pencil marks, and that often turns another cell into a single, like the 5 in Figure 6.

Printable Grids

We made two grids you can print and use with any puzzle. You can copy one from a newspaper, a book, or an app, or make up your own. Both are free PDFs sized for Letter paper, with the grid as large as a home printer allows.

  • Blank Sudoku Grid (PDF): an empty grid with thick lines around each box.
  • Sudoku Grid With Candidates (PDF): the same grid, with the numbers 1 to 9 in small circles at the top of every cell.

The candidates grid saves you from writing pencil marks by hand. It works like a fill-in-the-bubble test. As you rule out a number for a cell, strike out its circle or fill it in. When only one circle is left, write the number in the open space below.

When you print, choose "Actual size" or "100%" instead of "Fit to page", so the grid stays full size.

If you make your own puzzles, keep in mind that a well-made puzzle has only one answer. A homemade puzzle might have more than one, and that can make it hard to solve by logic alone. Copying a published puzzle is the easiest way to begin.

The TLDR

  • Sudoku is a logic puzzle, and it needs no math.
  • Every row, column, and box must hold the numbers 1 to 9, each only once.
  • A well-made puzzle has one answer, so you never need to guess.
  • Use a pencil, start with the easiest technique, and go back to it after every new number.
  • When a group has one empty cell left, fill in the missing number.
  • Cross-hatch by tracing one number's rows and columns through a box.
  • Count the numbers in a cell's row, column, and box; if eight are taken, the ninth is the answer.
  • Pencil marks help you find a number that fits in only one cell of a group.
  • Two cells with the same two pencil marks let you erase those numbers from the rest of the group.
  • Print the free grids at actual size, and use the candidates grid to strike out numbers as you go.
