To the Members of Parliament,

I am writing as a Canadian who buys video games, and on behalf of everyone who has paid for a game only to learn it was never really theirs. I am asking Parliament to take up a practical framework for digital game ownership: one that guarantees a purchased game stays playable, can be retrieved again, and can be played on any platform where the same game is sold.

What I Am Asking of You

  1. Sponsor a House of Commons e-petition calling on the Government to study digital game ownership and preservation.
  2. Raise the issue in committee, with the committees responsible for heritage and for industry, and with the ministers responsible for Library and Archives Canada and for consumer protection.
  3. Introduce or co-sponsor a private member's bill built on the framework below. A detailed draft is published alongside this letter.

The Problem

When a Canadian "buys" a digital game, they receive a licence the seller controls. That licence can be revoked, the game can be switched off when servers close, and it cannot be played on another platform without buying it again.

  • Physical copies are disappearing. The boxed edition of Grand Theft Auto VI (November 2026) contains a download code instead of a disc (Croft, 2026). The second-hand market, and the last practical form of ownership, goes with it.
  • Purchased games are being shut off. Ubisoft's shutdown of The Crew left buyers with nothing playable and started the international Stop Killing Games movement.
  • Europe declined to act. The EU citizens' initiative gathered 1,294,188 verified signatures, but on 16 June 2026 the European Commission declined to propose binding legislation and opted for industry dialogue instead (European Commission, 2026).

Canada has a chance to lead where Europe stopped short.

The Proposal in Five Points

  1. A purchase is a real licence. Buying a game grants a perpetual, irrevocable, platform-independent licence to the title, not to one store's copy of it.
  2. A national archive and registry. A single public body keeps a record of what each registered buyer owns and stores playable copies of every major version, available only to people who bought the game until copyright expires.
  3. Play anywhere, within reason. A registered licence is honoured on every platform where the vendor sells that title, with one active session at a time, and the game can run without a launcher.
  4. Opt-in for players, mandatory for sellers. Players choose whether to register. Sellers must offer registration at checkout on no less favourable terms, and must honour it.
  5. A separate regulator with no access to identities. Enforcement sits with a body that never sees who owns what. It acts on opt-in crash reports and consumer complaints, with graduated penalties.

Why Canada, Why Now

Canada already has the building blocks. This framework extends existing law rather than inventing a new field.

  • Interoperability is already recognized. Bill C-294 (assented to November 7, 2024) amended the Copyright Act to allow circumventing a technological protection measure to make a program interoperable with another device or program (Government of Canada, 2024).
  • National preservation already exists. Under legal deposit, Library and Archives Canada collects Canadian publications intended for sale or public distribution (Library and Archives Canada, 2025). Games, however, are deposited only on written request from the Librarian and Archivist (Government of Canada, 2006); making game deposit mandatory, and playable, is a natural first step.
  • Competition law has been modernized for digital markets. The 2022–2024 Competition Act amendments broadened the abuse of dominance provisions with the digital economy in mind (Murray, 2024).

The European decision leaves an open question that a Canadian proposal can answer with a workable design.

What Industry Gets

This is not an all-or-nothing demand. The framework builds in compromises that address the industry's main objections:

Compromise What it protects for vendors
One active session per licence Prevents one purchase being shared across a household
Archive copies stored encrypted A breach does not leak playable games
Archive access only for buyers until copyright expires No free distribution of games still on sale
Physical discs stay outside the registry unless their code is redeemed No duplicate claims from resold discs
Vendors self-certify archived versions No government testing department; disputes only go to independent labs
Live online services remain the vendor's Only the playable snapshot is guaranteed, not the servers

The framework also gives vendors one trusted national record of ownership, which reduces fraud and support disputes.

How Canadians Can Help

  • Write to your own MP. The House of Commons website finds your MP by postal code. A short note saying you support this framework counts.
  • Share this letter with anyone who has lost a game they paid for.
  • Sign the e-petition if one is published.

Closing

What people buy should be theirs to keep and theirs to move. The tools to guarantee that already exist: digital signatures, national archives, and interoperability law. What has been missing is a framework that puts them together. I am asking you to help build it.

Respectfully,

J. Tuffner
