Digital Game Ownership Act: Proposal and Draft Bill

2026-11-23 · by J. Tuffner & Claude Opus 5.5

“Written by an average Joe and an above-average AI, or the other way around. Who knows? Either way, trust, but verify.”

This is a working mock-up of a Canadian federal statute guaranteeing that a purchased digital game stays playable, retrievable and portable. It is a policy draft, not legal advice, and would need review by legislative counsel before introduction.

Scope. The framework addresses two problems: purchased games being disabled or revoked, and purchased games being locked to one platform. It covers digital purchases, and physical copies only where their owner redeems a one-time registration code. Resale of digital licences is out of scope for this draft.

Drafting conventions. Square brackets mark items still under negotiation, with the opening position first. "Drafting note" paragraphs explain intent and are not bill text.

Problem 1: Purchased Games Can Be Taken Away

A digital game purchase grants a licence the vendor can revoke, alter or render unusable by shutting down servers. Ubisoft's shutdown of The Crew left buyers with no playable product. Physical media no longer offers an escape: the boxed edition of Grand Theft Auto VI contains only a download code (Croft, 2026), which ends resale and keeps control with the vendor.

Patches can also change a game after purchase. Buyers have no right to the version they paid for.

Problem 2: Purchased Games Are Locked to One Platform

The major storefronts (Steam, Epic, Ubisoft, Blizzard, Xbox, PlayStation, EA) compete with one another, but each keeps buyers inside its own ecosystem. The issue is lock-in rather than monopoly in the legal sense, which matters: abuse-of-dominance law requires proving one firm is dominant, while a portability rule does not.

  • On PC, the same Windows game is largely the same files across stores, yet each requires its own launcher, sometimes stacked. DRM-free stores show launchers are not technically necessary.
  • Across consoles, versions are separate ports, so a buyer pays again for the same title within one console generation. Xbox Play Anywhere shows cross-platform entitlement works when one company owns both stores.
  • On Linux, vendors can block whole operating systems. EA blocked Apex Legends on Linux, citing cheating (EA_Mako, n.d.).

Existing Law and the Gaps

Instrument What it does Gap this framework fills
Copyright Act, s. 41.12, as amended by Bill C-294 (2024) Permits circumventing a TPM to achieve interoperability Permits but does not require portability
Competition Act, abuse of dominance (2022–2024 amendments) Broadened, with digital-economy factors and private access Requires a dominant firm; lock-in by competing firms falls outside it
Library and Archives of Canada Act, legal deposit National collection of Canadian publications Games are deposited only on written request from the Librarian and Archivist (Regulations); no playability requirement, and foreign games not covered
Provincial consumer protection (e.g. Ontario Consumer Protection Act) Governs sale terms and disclosures No rules on licence revocation or portability
EU: Commission reply to "Stop Destroying Videogames" (16 June 2026) Declined binding legislation; industry dialogue No legal guarantee anywhere

Drafting note: jurisdiction. Consumer contracts are largely provincial in Canada. A federal bill would most safely rest on federal heads of power: copyright (licence terms and archive exceptions), Library and Archives Canada (the archive), and trade and commerce or competition (cross-platform obligations). Checkout rules may need a parallel provincial model act. Constitutional counsel should confirm.

Guiding Principles

  1. If you paid for it, it is yours to keep and yours to move.
  2. Own the title, not the build. Rights attach to the game, not to one platform's code.
  3. Opt-in for players, mandatory for sellers.
  4. Privacy by structure. Identity, ownership and enforcement are held by separate parties; no single body can see who owns what without the owner's request.
  5. Guarantee the snapshot, negotiate the service. Playable files are guaranteed; live online services remain the vendor's.
  6. Necessity and proportionality. Any barrier a vendor imposes must be necessary, proportionate and the least restrictive option.
  7. Graduated enforcement. Notice, then fines, then suspension of sales.

Draft Bill Text

An Act respecting the ownership, preservation and portability of digital video games

Short Title

1 This Act may be cited as the Digital Game Ownership Act.

Purpose

2 The purpose of this Act is to ensure that a person who purchases a video game may continue to play it, may retrieve it in a playable state, and may play it on any platform on which the same title is sold, while protecting the privacy of purchasers and the legitimate interests of rights holders.

Interpretation

3 The following definitions apply in this Act.

  • archive means the National Game Archive and Registry established under section 4.
  • regulator means the Digital Game Ownership Commissioner appointed under section 20.
  • vendor means a person who sells, licenses or distributes a video game to a purchaser in Canada, including a publisher, developer, platform holder or storefront operator.
  • title means a video game as a work, regardless of the platform, build or version through which it is delivered, and includes any content sold with or as part of it, including bonus, edition and pre-order content.
  • title licence means the licence described in section 9.
  • registered purchase means a purchase of a title recorded in the ledger under section 6.
  • key means a cryptographic key, held by a purchaser, that identifies a set of registered purchases without identifying the purchaser.
  • snapshot means a complete copy of a title at a fixed version, deposited under section 13, that is playable without the vendor's live service.
  • live service means online functions operated on servers controlled by the vendor, including matchmaking, persistent online worlds, storefronts and events.
  • reference platform means the operating system, hardware range and dependencies on which a snapshot is certified to run.
  • launcher means software, other than the title itself, that must run in order to start the title.

Part 1: National Game Archive and Registry

4 (1) There is established a body to be known as the National Game Archive and Registry. [Option: established within Library and Archives Canada.]

(2) The archive shall maintain (a) a ledger of registered purchases; (b) a repository of snapshots; (c) an identity vault; and (d) a public interface through which vendors and purchasers may verify title licences.

5 The ledger shall record only that a key holds a title licence for a title. It shall not record a purchaser's name, address, payment details or play activity.

6 A purchase is registered when the purchaser elects registration at checkout, at code redemption, or at a later time under section 8, and the vendor transmits the purchase to the archive.

7 (1) The identity vault shall record only the link between a purchaser's identity and their key, for the purpose of key recovery.

(2) The identity vault and the ledger shall be held separately and shall be joined only at the request of the key holder, and every such join shall be logged and disclosed to the key holder.

(3) No vendor or regulator shall have access to the identity vault.

8 A purchaser may register a past purchase on presentation of the vendor's transaction record. [Option A: purchases made on or after the coming into force of this Act. Option B: any purchase for which a vendor holds a record.]

Drafting note. The archive is funded through [a parliamentary appropriation / a levy per registered sale / both], fixed in statute so that the archive's existence does not depend on annual policy decisions. Snapshots are stored encrypted and in geographically separate locations.

Part 2: Rights of Registered Purchasers

9 A registered purchase confers a perpetual, irrevocable, non-exclusive, platform-independent licence to use the title for personal use ("title licence").

10 The holder of a title licence has the right (a) to run the title locally, offline and without a launcher; (b) to retrieve from the archive any snapshot of the title; (c) to run any snapshot, including an earlier version; and (d) to play the title on any platform on which the vendor sells that title within the same hardware generation, without further payment.

11 A title licence may be used in one active session at a time.

12 On the expiry of copyright in a title, the archive shall make its snapshots available to the public.

Part 3: Obligations of Vendors

13 (1) A vendor shall deposit a snapshot of a title with the archive (a) at launch, including any patch required on the day of release; (b) after each major change; (c) at least once in each year in which the title is updated; and (d) before ending any live service on which the title depends.

(2) Each snapshot shall be accompanied by its reference platform.

14 A change is a major change if it (a) removes content included in the title; (b) materially alters core gameplay, progression or in-game economy; or (c) alters the terms on which the title was sold, including by making an offline function dependent on a live service. A change that only fixes defects, improves performance or adjusts quality of life is not a major change. The vendor's own version label is not determinative.

15 A vendor shall certify, on deposit, that a snapshot runs on its reference platform without the live service.

16 (1) A vendor that sells a title in Canada shall offer the purchaser the option to register the purchase, and the option shall be offered on terms no less favourable than an unregistered purchase, including as to price, content, timing and prominence.

(2) The option shall be presented as a neutral choice, in a form prescribed by the regulator, with no option preselected.

(3) A purchaser may make a standing election through a linked archive account, which applies to all subsequent purchases until changed.

(4) No vendor shall advertise or communicate in a manner intended to discourage registration.

17 (1) Where a title delivered on physical media connects to a vendor's service, the vendor shall include with each copy a unique one-time registration code, activated at the point of sale.

(2) Each time such a title starts with a network connection and is unregistered, it shall offer registration in the form prescribed under subsection 16(2), with an option not to be asked again, which the purchaser may reverse.

(3) A redeemed code may not be redeemed again. The physical copy remains usable as before.

18 (1) A vendor shall honour a title licence on every platform on which it sells the title within the same hardware generation.

(2) No vendor shall require a launcher, or a second launcher, to run a title offline.

(3) No vendor shall impose a technical restriction that prevents a title licence holder from exercising a right under section 10 unless the vendor demonstrates that the restriction is necessary, proportionate and the least restrictive means available.

(4) A restriction imposed to prevent cheating may apply to online multiplayer functions only, and shall not prevent the use of functions that do not require a live service.

19 (1) A title licence may be revoked only for fraud, a chargeback or a refund, on application by the vendor to the regulator stating the ground.

(2) The key holder shall be notified and may dispute the revocation before it takes effect.

(3) No vendor may alter the ledger directly.

Part 4: Digital Game Ownership Commissioner

20 (1) The Governor in Council shall appoint a Digital Game Ownership Commissioner, independent of the archive.

(2) The regulator shall have no access to the identity vault and may query the ledger only on a request signed by the key holder.

21 (1) The regulator shall receive crash reports submitted voluntarily by players. A report may contain only the crash log, title version, snapshot identifier, operating system and hardware specification.

(2) No report shall contain a key, name, purchase history or any other personal information.

22 (1) Where reports indicate that a snapshot fails on its reference platform [at a threshold prescribed by regulation], the regulator shall notify the vendor.

(2) Where the vendor disputes the notice, the regulator shall refer the matter to an accredited independent testing laboratory, at the vendor's cost if the failure is confirmed.

23 (1) A vendor that fails to remedy a confirmed failure, or that contravenes Part 3, is liable to (a) notice with a deadline to remedy of [30] days; (b) an administrative monetary penalty for each day after the deadline; and (c) suspension of sales of the title in Canada until the contravention is remedied.

(2) A patch that remedies a contravention or addresses a security vulnerability is never subject to suspension.

24 A key holder may complain to the regulator that a right under this Act has been denied. The regulator shall verify the complainant's control of the key by a cryptographic challenge, without requiring the complainant's identity.

Part 5: Offline Licences and Keys

25 A key holder may bind an offline title licence to (a) a registered device; or (b) a portable hardware token conforming to an open standard prescribed by regulation.

26 A key holder may rebind an offline licence to a new device or token. Rebinding without the previous device is permitted [up to a number of times per year prescribed by regulation], with further requests by application.

27 [Option A: an offline licence does not expire. Option B: an offline licence remains valid for one year and renews automatically whenever the device or token connects to the archive.]

28 A renewal transmits only the token or device identifier and proof of a valid signature, and receives only an updated expiry and a cancellation list. No play activity or device information shall be collected.

Part 6: General

29 Any term of a contract that purports to waive or limit a right under this Act is void.

30 The Minister shall cause a review of this Act to be conducted within five years after it comes into force.

31 This Act comes into force on a day fixed by order of the Governor in Council, being not later than [two] years after royal assent.

Anticipated Objections

Objection Severity Response in this draft
Curtails developer choice; publishers' trade body made this argument against Stop Killing Games (Bonifield, 2025) Major Live services remain the vendor's; only the snapshot is guaranteed
Rights holders should decide how works are used, the EU Commission's reasoning Major Licence terms are set at sale; the Act defines the minimum a sale must include
Cost of cross-platform play when another platform keeps the sale Major Limited to one hardware generation; revenue arrangements between platforms left to negotiation
Piracy from an archive of master copies Major Encrypted storage; access only for licence holders until copyright expires
Cheating on open systems such as Linux Minor Anti-cheat may restrict multiplayer only, subject to a proportionality test
Federal jurisdiction over consumer sales Major Rely on copyright, archive and trade and commerce powers; parallel provincial model act
Foreign vendors may restrict sales in Canada Major Build as a model compatible with future EU or other national registries
Government database of purchases Minor (opt-in) Split ledger and identity vault; regulator has no identity access

Open Decisions

  • Physical copies: confirm the one-time activated code mechanism (s. 17) and how disc-based titles join the registry in practice
  • Resale of digital licences: undecided; would require transfers between keys inside the ledger
  • Past purchases: Option A or B in s. 8
  • Remedy deadline for a broken snapshot (s. 23, currently 30 days)
  • Offline renewal: Option A or B in s. 27, held as a bargaining chip
  • Rebinding limit per year (s. 26)
  • Crash-report threshold (s. 22)
  • Archive home: standalone body or within Library and Archives Canada (s. 4)
  • Funding model: appropriation, per-sale levy, or both
  • Cross-platform revenue: whether the Act sets any rule or leaves it to platforms
